# Skin Lesion Guide — Simple Patient-Friendly Information

This guide explains seven skin-lesion categories in everyday language. It is for general awareness only—not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan.

> **Important:** An AI image prediction cannot confirm whether a spot is harmless or cancerous. A doctor or dermatologist may need to examine it and sometimes perform a biopsy. Get medical advice for any new, changing, bleeding, painful, or non-healing spot.

---

## 1. AK — Actinic Keratosis

### 1. What actually is it?
Actinic keratosis is a rough, dry or scaly patch of skin caused by long-term sun damage. It often appears on sun-exposed areas such as the face, ears, scalp, hands, and arms. It is not usually skin cancer, but some patches can develop into a type of skin cancer, so they should be checked.

### 2. Why it may have caused
The main reason is repeated exposure to ultraviolet (UV) rays from sunlight over many years. Outdoor work, frequent sunburn, and a weakened immune system can increase the risk.

### 3. What to do now
- Arrange a doctor or dermatologist visit to confirm what the patch is.
- A doctor may treat it with freezing, prescription creams, light treatment, or a minor procedure.
- Use sun protection, such as shade, protective clothing, and sunscreen.
- Get it checked promptly if it thickens, grows, hurts, or bleeds.

---

## 2. BCC — Basal Cell Carcinoma

### 1. What actually is it?
Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a type of skin cancer. It often looks like a shiny or pearly bump, a pink patch, or a sore that keeps forming a scab and bleeding. It usually grows slowly and rarely spreads to distant parts of the body, but it can damage nearby skin and tissue if left untreated.

### 2. Why it may have caused
Long-term exposure to sunlight and UV rays is the most common cause. Risk can also be higher after repeated sunburn, with older age, or when the immune system is weakened.

### 3. What to do now
- Consult a dermatologist promptly—do not leave a suspicious growth untreated.
- Treatment commonly involves removing the cancer. Depending on the case, a doctor may use surgery, specialised surgery, creams, or radiotherapy.
- Early treatment is usually very successful and often cures BCC.
- Protect your skin from further sun damage and attend follow-up checks if advised.

## 3. BKL — Benign Keratosis-Like Lesions

### 1. What actually is it?
BKL is a category used for skin spots or growths that often look like harmless, waxy, rough, or slightly raised patches. Some may look as if they are sitting on top of the skin; others are flat brown spots. The category includes different conditions, so the exact type may need a doctor's assessment.

### 2. Why it may have caused
Some common examples become more frequent with age. Sun exposure can contribute to certain brown spots, while other growths may develop as part of normal skin changes. The cause depends on the specific type.

### 3. What to do now
- If a doctor confirms that it is harmless and it is not bothering you, treatment may not be needed.
- If it gets irritated, itches, or you want it removed, a doctor may freeze or remove it.
- Do not cut, scrape, or burn it off at home.
- Have a new, rapidly changing, very dark, painful, or bleeding spot checked—some skin cancers can look similar.

---

## 4. DF — Dermatofibroma

### 1. What actually is it?
A dermatofibroma is a small, firm, usually harmless bump in the skin. It often appears on the legs and may be pink, light brown, or darker brown. Some bumps dip slightly in the middle when the surrounding skin is gently pinched.

### 2. Why it may have caused
It may develop after a minor skin injury, such as an insect bite or a small cut, although the exact cause is often unclear. It is not usually caused by anything dangerous.

### 3. What to do now
- A confirmed dermatofibroma usually does not need treatment.
- If it is painful, repeatedly irritated, or its diagnosis is uncertain, consult a dermatologist.
- A doctor may remove it or take a small sample if needed. Removal can leave a scar, and the bump may sometimes return.
- Get a new or changing bump checked rather than assuming it is a dermatofibroma.

---

## 5. MEL — Melanoma

### 1. What actually is it?
Melanoma is a serious type of skin cancer that starts in the cells responsible for skin colour. It can appear in an existing mole or as a new spot. It may be dark, uneven in colour, or sometimes pink or red. Melanoma can grow deeper and spread to other parts of the body, but treatment is often much more successful when it is found early.

### 2. Why it may have caused
Melanoma can be linked to UV exposure from sunlight or tanning beds, especially repeated sunburn. Risk may also be higher with a family history of melanoma, many moles, or certain unusual moles. It can occur even without these risk factors.

### 3. What to do now
- **Consult the nearest dermatologist at the earliest if melanoma is suspected. Do not delay medical evaluation.**
- A dermatologist may examine the spot and recommend a biopsy to confirm what it is.
- Treatment often involves surgery; depending on whether it has spread, specialist treatments such as immunotherapy or targeted medicines may also be needed.
- Do not rely on an AI prediction, photo, or lack of pain to rule melanoma out.

**Warning signs:** a spot that changes in size, shape, or colour; has uneven edges; looks different from other moles; bleeds; or does not heal should be checked promptly.

## 6. NV — Melanocytic Nevus (Mole)

### 1. What actually is it?
A melanocytic nevus, commonly called a mole, is a usually harmless spot made from pigment-producing skin cells. Moles can be flat or raised and may be brown, black, pink, or close to your skin colour. Most remain harmless.

### 2. Why it may have caused
Moles develop when pigment-producing cells grow together in one area. Their number can be influenced by age, family history, skin type, and sun exposure. Many appear during childhood or young adulthood.

### 3. What to do now
- A stable, ordinary mole usually does not need treatment.
- If it changes, looks unusual, bleeds, itches, or hurts, arrange a dermatologist visit.
- A doctor may remove it or take a biopsy if there is concern.
- Avoid trying to remove a mole at home.

---

## 7. VASC — Vascular Lesions

### 1. What actually is it?
Vascular lesions are skin marks or bumps involving blood vessels. A common example is a cherry angioma—a small, smooth red or purple spot that is usually harmless. Other vascular lesions can look different, so this category does not describe just one condition.

### 2. Why it may have caused
Some vascular spots, such as cherry angiomas, become more common with age. They form when small blood vessels cluster in the skin. The cause of other vascular lesions depends on their type.

### 3. What to do now
- A typical, unchanged cherry angioma usually does not need treatment.
- If it repeatedly bleeds, becomes painful, grows quickly, or its appearance is uncertain, consult a doctor.
- If removal is needed, a doctor may use laser treatment, freezing, or electrocautery.
- Do not puncture or remove a red or purple spot yourself.

---

## A simple reminder

Many skin spots are harmless, but appearance alone cannot reliably identify them. If a spot is new, changing, bleeding, painful, or not healing, have it checked by a qualified healthcare professional. **A computer-generated result is not a medical diagnosis.**
